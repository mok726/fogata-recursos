# Huerta y Compost en la Escuela

## Resumen
Cultivar la propia comida ayuda a desperdiciar menos: cuando sabemos el trabajo que costó, nos cuesta más tirarla. Además, ayuda a comer más sano, atrae polinizadores, aumenta la biodiversidad y es una actividad linda y relajante al aire libre. El compost mantiene sana la tierra y evita que los restos de comida vayan al basural. Es un proyecto largo, en etapas y con mucho trabajo en equipo. El original lo plantea en la escuela, pero sirve igual para el local del grupo o la casa.

## Desarrollo

### 1. Planificar
Formen una comisión y consigan, con la escuela o el grupo, un lugar para la huerta. Revisen guías paso a paso (por ejemplo, la de la FAO para huertas escolares, o en Argentina, por ejemplo, el programa ProHuerta del INTA). Si hay jardinero, pregúntenle qué verduras van mejor con el clima y el suelo; si no, consulten en un vivero.

### 2. Plantar
- Que la huerta tenga al menos 6 horas de sol por día y que las plantas altas no tapen a las bajas.
- La mejor tierra tiene mucho compost y materia orgánica: al apretarla se une, pero se desarma fácil. Así retiene agua sin encharcarse.
- La mayoría de las verduras necesita unos 2,5 cm de agua por semana, contando la lluvia.
- Si pueden, usen *mulch* (cobertura de material orgánico): mantiene la humedad, mejora la tierra y controla las malezas.
- Eviten los insecticidas, que dañan a muchos seres vivos que no son plaga. Prueben repelentes naturales, como el aceite de neem.
- Planten flores entre las verduras para atraer polinizadores. Algunas, como las copetes (tagetes), además alejan plagas.

### 3. Compostar
- Armen una compostera: un tacho viejo, un cajón de madera o una comprada. Ubíquenla cerca de la huerta, en un lugar discreto.
- Junten restos: cáscaras de papa y banana, saquitos de té, yerba y café usados, cáscaras de huevo, flores viejas, hojas, ramitas.
- Pongan capas de **verdes** (restos de fruta y verdura, café, pasto cortado), que aportan nitrógeno, y **marrones** (maples de huevo, diario, hojas secas, agujas de pino), que aportan carbono. Conviene cortar el papel en tiras.
- Cada semana más o menos, revuelvan la pila para que entre aire. Puede tardar unos meses.
- Cuando esté listo, mézclenlo con la tierra de la huerta.

## Cierre
¡Disfruten las verduras! Cuenten lo que hicieron e inviten a otros a sumarse.
- ¿Qué fue lo más difícil de sostener?
- ¿Cuánto resto de comida dejó de ir a la basura gracias a la compostera?

**English version:**

C.2 CULTIVATING & COMPOSTING @ SCHOOL (LEVEL 1, 2, 3)

AIM: Learn how to start and care for a school garden.
TIME NEEDED: This long-term activity involves a lot of group work and several stages.
MATERIALS: For the vegetable garden: * A dedicated space in your school where you can start growing vegetables. * Raised beds. * Seedlings. * Watering cans, trowels, shovels, water hose with spray nozzle, gardening gloves, twine. For the compost bin: * Compost pail. * Dried plant materials, such as fallen leaves, twigs, small branches, newspaper, and wood shavings. * Garden fork to stir the compost with.

BACKGROUND
Growing one's food can help tackle FLW. People tend to care more about waste when growing their own food and are aware of the effort that went into cultivating it. Growing one's food also helps people follow healthy diets. It helps the environment by encouraging pollinators to swing by and boosting local biodiversity. Looking after a vegetable garden can also be an enjoyable and relaxing group activity that can encourage a love for nature and allows participants to spend time outdoors. Garden composting is another important activity that keeps the soil healthy and saves food scraps from landfills.
LEARN MORE AT: * Setting up and running a school garden (FAO): www.fao.org/3/i1118e/i1118e.pdf. * www.usda.gov/media/blog/2013/08/13/start-school-garden-heres-how. * https://learn.eartheasy.com/guides/how-to-start-a-school-garden-your-complete-guide/. * www.growveg.com/guides/how-to-set-up-a-school-garden.

ACTIVITY
1. Start planning. Start by forming a committee and work with school authorities to secure a spot for your vegetable garden. Review the guides in the Background and suggested resources above for step-by-step ideas on how to start the garden. If your school has gardeners, talk to them about the best vegetables to grow for your climate and soil. Otherwise, talk to local experts at a garden centre or nursery.
2. Start planting. Start your garden, look after it and enjoy watching your vegetables grow. Get help from your school gardeners or local garden centre staff. Here are some basic tips:
* Ensure your vegetable patch gets at least six hours of full sun each day. Be careful that taller plants do not shade the shorter ones.
* The best soil for vegetables has lots of compost and organic matter. Mix organic material in your soil and make sure the final mix binds together when squeezed but breaks apart easily. This way, it will retain water easily but won't become waterlogged.
* Most vegetable plants require one inch of water per week, including rainfall.
* If possible, use mulch, a layer of (usually) organic material placed on the soil's surface that helps it stay moist, improve the health and fertility of the soil, and help control weeds.
* Avoid insecticides, if possible, as these can harm many creatures that are not pests and can damage soil and air quality. Try to opt for natural pest repellents, such as neem spray.
3. Start composting.
* Create a compost bin, such as an old trash bin or a wooden box. You can also buy a compost bin online. Set it up in an area close to your garden, preferably out of sight.
* Start collecting food scraps such as potato skins, banana peels, tea bags, coffee grounds, eggshells, old flowers, leaves, twigs, etc. Store them in a container so you can add them bit by bit to your compost bin, as you will need to layer your compost.
* When adding compost to your bin, layer it according to greens and browns. According to npr.org, "Greens" are typically food scraps, like fruit and vegetable peelings, coffee grounds, or, if you have a yard, grass clippings. These add nitrogen – a crucial element for microbial growth. Microorganisms are the true heroes of this process; they do the heavy lifting of decomposition. "Browns" are more carbon-rich – think egg cartons, newspapers, dried leaves, and pine needles. It helps to shred the paper products before putting them in your pile."
* Wait for your compost to decompose, which could take a few months. Every week or so, get a stick or a spade and turn your pile around to keep the air flowing.
* Once your compost is ready, add it to the soil in your vegetable garden.
Enjoy your fresh, school-grown vegetables! Spread the word and invite others to join in the effort.
LET FLOWERS HELP: Planting flowers among your fruits and vegetables will attract pollinators, which will help all your plants grow and benefits the overall ecosystem, contributing to a greener city. Flowers such as marigolds ward off pests, too.
